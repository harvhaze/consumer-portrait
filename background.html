Background & Methods

Meta and others collect and trade data from their userbase. This project is an attempt to narrow down where the company has calculated and miscalculated the consumer habits and identity of a single Instagram user (myself).

Targeted ads tell two stories (which inform each other symbiotically and parasitically): how an individual uses the internet and the assumptions algorithms make about an individual based on their internet history (and personal information, perhaps including biometrics, health data, and private conversations).

Targeted ads have the potential to reinforce social boundaries through dictating identity, manipulating consumer behavior, and even exposing secrets. Back in the early 2010s, Target used shopping trends to identify and market directly to first trimester pregnant women, which ultimately led to the exposure of a teen pregnancy.

Data is more and more aggressively weaponized against internet users in the United States (and non-users globally), and increasingly with carceral or fatal consequences.

In order to access vital resources, we to some degree must feed personal financial and health data to external agencies, and for convenience many of us volunteer additional information. Once we have offered this data, we have no knowledge or control over who access it.

One metric through which to ascertain this is how an identity is constructed through targeted ads.

Google and Meta allow users to peek behind the curtain and even hide or rewrite parts of the constructed identity narrative used to tailor advertisements, but what is viewable and editable is not the whole picture.

As a side effect, this personal archive also serves as a snapshot of internet commerce and culture, as a whole. The collection period for material was over the course of a 3-week period during April and May of 2026. It is designed to be a fixed collection; the site has been developed in such a manner to host and display this data set with no intention of expansion. This is not every ad the user was shown during the collection period, but it is very close to it. Intentionally left out of the collection were ads which the user had seen before the start of the project and ads understood to be ubiquitous across platforms and userbase. I.e., if the ad was also running on cable television at the time, it would not be included in the curated set. Additionally, ads for which the product was not readily clear were not included.

Because there is no straightforward way for Instagram users to download content (as opposed to other social media sites such as Facebook, X, Reddit, Tumblr, etc. which allow images to be saved), the collected images here have no attributes of the original file. Each artifact is a screenshot (JPEG) which has been cropped to only include the name of the account and the image content of the advertisement, omitting time-based attributes and other information on the screen including captions. Screenshotting allows social media users to capture, isolate, and repurpose other users' content instantaneously, almost without even pausing their infinite scroll. These screenshots are not produced at what is considered archival resolution. (Although not a direct interaction, I have a suspicion that Meta tracks the content users screenshot, which I believe over the course of the experiment impacted my marketing algorithm, creating something of an echo chamber.) Each JPEG has descriptive alt text which begins with the @ handle of the original post account and a visual description of the image. It is noted if the image is a still captured from a video.

Each artifact is assigned a table which includes detailed demographic attributes I, the author of the project, understand to be the intended audience of the ad. This does not necessarily mean who I think can or should use the product, but rather how I understand heuristics and visual innuendo. This is a flawed exercise, of course. My own lived experiences, biases, and insecurities heavily inform the construction of this metadata.

A more compelling and effective exercise might be to prompt Meta AI find out what it interprets as the target audience for each advertisement and surface the machine's biases, but due to ethical constraints I have opted instead to accept my own biases as part of the project's meaning.

The original intention of the project was to understand how algorithms approximate queer and trans identity. The trends that emerge from the collection demonstrate that marketing algorithms determine my gender and expression to be much more feminine and overt than resonate with my own experience of myself. Through cross-referencing personality traits and interests with trends in gender and sexual identities that emerge, I see a mirror which reflects how my information behaviors are interpreted as a performance of identity.

Something I struggled with a lot was consistency and syntax in the development of a controlled vocabulary, and how I associated certain tags in different contexts. The tags are color coded to communicate if I determine the attribute is true, partially true, or false about me. As I was compiling my data for the profile, I noticed that I had color coded the same attribute as different colors in reference to different items. This is not necessarily due to my own waivering of self-identification, but rather how different messages evoke a range of emotional responses. Additionally, some tags such as “upper middle class” and “upper middle income” or “films” and “film buff” may seem interchangable and could possibly benefit from consolidation, but have some key nuances that I think were worth maintaining for the exercise.

In an early iteration I included race/ethnicity as a metadata field. It certainly informs the marketing algorithm output, likely heavily so, and I would be remiss not to acknowledge it. The reason I quickly abandoned its inclusion in the metadata was that it became apparent that I was ill-equipped to predict the nuanced ways race played a role in how ads were selected for me. Prior to abandoning it (which was after cataloguing about 15 artifacts), I realized I was putting “n/a” in the race/ethnicity field for virtually each artifact. I knew it was somehow more of a factor than was communicated, but I could not readily express how whiteness would be seen as an attribute associated with the product, without myself perpetuating stereotypes. I found myself asking, am I being shown this product because it is being pushed regardless of the user's race, or is it because the algorithm sees it as a product for white people? In a social framework which views whiteness as neutral or default, and with a sample pool of 1, I could not answer that question. The alt text (which serves as a form of metadata) for each image does note skin tone, ethnicity, and/or race where they are visually depicted.

Meta has a feature which allows users to see what activity prompted the algorithm to show the ad, but my experience of it is incoherent and dissonent. For example, I was shown an ad for a pediatric charity because I “interacted with ads about travel, apparel, and movies & television". This information does not inform me why the algorithm thought I would be interested in the charity, and these are broad topics that surely every social media user interacts with to some degree. There is an illusion of transparency, but no real information is actually communicated.

Through a special request, a ZIP folder can be sent to your email of your entire account information and history, including ads that you have clicked on and other sites tracking your data. However, still no demographic profile is included. The information (which we, users, already know because we are the ones who generated it) is there, but Meta still does not disclose how it is interpreted.

Images are displayed as a horizontal scroll to be treated more like artifacts, rather than to mimic the vertical scroll of the platform and interrupting the monotonous stream of content.
